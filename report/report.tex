\section{Dataset Documentation}

\subsection{Data Description}
The dataset comprises survey responses from a class cohort regarding 60 Likert-scale
statements spanning four key topics: Technology (T), Education (E), Society/Ethics (S),
and Environment (V).

\subsection{Encoding and Preprocessing}
Standard deviation rescaling (without mean shift) was applied to the raw response matrix. This corrects for the difference between
``extreme'' and ``mild'' responders without destroying the genuine uniform agreement signal present across the class. Cosine similarity was used
as the primary metric on the rescaled matrix as, unlike full z-scoring, which collapses neutral opinions, it preserves directional agreement
effectively.

\subsection{Handling Missing Data and Data Quality}
The initial dataset contained 96 respondents. Nine respondents with more than 50\%
missing data (nodes 30, 39, 44, 60, 68, 73, 77, 78, 87) were dropped to ensure data
quality, yielding a robust final cohort of \textbf{87 respondents}. To prevent highly
skewed similarities based on only a handful of shared answers, a strict floor
(\texttt{MIN\_OVERLAP = 5}) was applied to pairwise comparisons: any pairwise similarity
computed from fewer than 5 jointly answered statements was discarded (\texttt{np.nan}).

Because survey similarities naturally vary in strength and reciprocity, the User--User
network was modeled using a deliberately designed \textbf{three-network framework}.
Instead of treating one graph as universally ``correct,'' three purpose-built views of
the same underlying similarity matrix were generated, each using \textbf{top-k
sparsification (k = 8)}, chosen because it produces a stable, fully connected base
structure in the directed case without artificially isolating nodes.

\textbf{Directed Top-8 Network (Primary Network).}
Edges are directed from user A to user B if B is in A's top 8 most similar peers. This
network acts as the base representation: cosine similarity's asymmetric top-k membership
is a genuine, informative feature of the data, capturing ``who looks up to whom'' in
opinion space. It preserves the full 694 edges and is used for PageRank, in/out-strength,
and connected-component metrics.

\textbf{Mutual Top-8 Network (High-Confidence Projection).}
Edges exist only if A and B mutually rank each other in their top 8. This projection
isolates reciprocal agreement, purposely shattering the network to reveal the deeply
connected ``consensus core'' (the largest component) versus the fragmented periphery.

\textbf{Non-Mutual Undirected Top-8 Network (Comparison Baseline).}
An edge exists if \emph{either} A or B is in the other's top 8. This network is used
specifically for cross-category and engagement-weighted comparisons. Enforcing mutuality
on thinner data subsets (like specific topics) fragments the graph unevenly, so the non-mutual approach guarantees a consistently
connected structure necessary for fair modularity and community comparisons.

\textit{Methodological implementations:} Louvain community detection was used; for the primary directed graph, edge weights for
reciprocal pairs were summed (weight-summed symmetrization) rather than dropped,
preserving the $\sim$78\% of network structure that is inherently one-directional.
NetworkX's Eigenvector Centrality \texttt{max\_iter} was raised to 1000 to ensure
convergence on sparse structures. For the signed network, raw similarity is heavily
right-skewed, so thresholds were dynamically calibrated to the extreme tails of the
distribution: positive threshold = 0.9 (exceptional similarity) and negative threshold
= $-0.1$ (exceptional dissimilarity).

This network flips the viewpoint of the User--User graph: instead of asking which
\emph{respondents} answered alike, it asks which \emph{statements} are answered alike by
the class. It starts from the same encoded, engagement-filtered, per-respondent
std-rescaled matrix $X$ of size $87\times 60$ (Section~3), with missing entries
kept as NaN (\texttt{src/statement\_network.py}).

\begin{enumerate}[nosep, leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Nodes.} One node per statement (60 in total), annotated with its T/E/S/V
    category and full text.
    \item \textbf{Similarity.} For every pair of statements $(i,j)$ we compute the Spearman
    rank correlation $r_{ij}$ between their two columns of $X$, using only the respondents who
    answered both (pairwise-complete). Pairs with fewer than 15 overlapping respondents
    (\texttt{MIN\_PERIODS = 15}) get no correlation. Spearman is used, rather than the cosine
    similarity of the User--User network, because the responses are ordinal and here we measure
    co-variation across respondents. It is computed on the \emph{rescaled} matrix because
    otherwise ``extreme'' responders (who mark almost everything Strongly Agree/Disagree) push
    every pair of columns towards the same corners and inflate the correlation between
    otherwise unrelated statements.
    \item \textbf{Edges.} An undirected edge $\{i,j\}$ is kept iff $|r_{ij}| \geq \tau = 0.30$
    (\texttt{EDGE\_THRESHOLD}), with weight $w_{ij} = r_{ij}$ so that the sign is preserved
    (positive = statements endorsed together, negative = opposing opinions).
    \item \textbf{Metrics.} \emph{Degree} counts neighbours (unweighted).
    \emph{Eigenvector centrality} is computed on $|w_{ij}|$, since it requires non-negative
    weights (\texttt{max\_iter} = 1000). \emph{Betweenness} is computed on the distance
    $d_{ij} = 1 - |r_{ij}|$, because NetworkX reads edge weights as traversal costs, and using
    $r$ directly would make a strong tie look ``farther'' than a weak one. Connectivity
    diagnostics (components, largest component, isolated statements) are also recorded, since
    at higher thresholds statements can silently lose all their edges.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Threshold choice.} The cutoff $\tau$ controls how dense the network is:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
$\tau$ & \textbf{Edges} & \textbf{Density} & \textbf{Negative edges} \\
\midrule
0.10 & 1{,}546 & 0.8734 & 31 \\
0.20 & 1{,}211 & 0.6842 & 5 \\
0.25 & 1{,}021 & 0.5768 & 3 \\
\textbf{0.30} & \textbf{810} & \textbf{0.4576} & \textbf{0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $\tau = 0.10$ the graph is almost complete (87.3\% of all pairs), so it barely
differentiates between statements. $\tau = 0.30$ gives a more selective network while still
retaining one large connected structure, and was used as the final network. Note that this
choice also removes every negative edge.

skipping $\neq$ a Neutral (0) response.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{User--User Network}

\textbf{A dominant consensus core.} A small, highly representative cluster of
respondents acts as the ideological center of the class. Respondents 81, 105, 110, 114,
and 67 consistently top the charts across both in-strength (most nominated as similar)
and PageRank in the Directed network. Nodes 81, 105, 94, 67, and 110 also top eigenvector
centrality in the Mutual network. They represent the ``class average,'' acting as
gravitational hubs in the opinion space.

\textbf{Hub-and-spoke agreement (low reciprocity).} Similarity in the class is
fundamentally directional. Only 21.9\% of directed edges are reciprocated. While the
entire class is weakly connected (100\% WCC), the largest strongly connected component
(SCC) is just 39\% of nodes (34 nodes). Most students ``point to'' the consensus core,
but the core does not point back. When mutuality is strictly enforced, the network
shatters into 49 micro-communities with 57 unconnected nodes, showing that broad opinion
camps are held together by mostly one-directional similarity.

\textbf{Polarization exists only at the extreme margins.} Using the strictly calibrated
signed network (thresholds 0.9 and $-0.1$), the extreme tails of agreement and
disagreement are relatively balanced but incredibly sparse: 60 positive edges vs.\ 47
negative edges out of thousands of possible pairs. Deep ideological opposites do exist
in the class, but they are rare, disagreement overall is moderate, there are no two big heavily polarized opinion groups.

\textbf{Opinion outliers.} Looking at bottom out-strength scores in the Directed graph
identifies individuals whose closest peers still aren't very
similar to them. Respondent 120 (out-strength 0.23) is a massive anomaly, scoring an
order of magnitude lower than the rest of the cohort (the next lowest is node 119 at
3.00). This respondent's response pattern is highly idiosyncratic compared to the
broader class consensus.

\textbf{Topic-driven factionalism.} While the class shows general consensus,
factionalism spikes depending on the subject matter. Topics related to Technology
(modularity 0.377, 5 communities) and Education (modularity 0.370, 7 communities)
display significantly higher modularity, meaning tighter, more distinct echo chambers,
than Environment (0.299) or Society (0.313). This suggests that Tech and Education
statements are where the most distinct dividing lines in class opinion are drawn.

\subsection{Statement--Statement Network}

\textbf{A dense, uniformly positive structure.} At $|r| \geq 0.30$, 810 of the 1{,}770
possible statement pairs (45.8\%) are linked (average degree 27 out of 59), and \emph{every}
retained edge is positive. Answers to many different statements therefore move together across
respondents, which points to substantial shared structure in the class's opinions rather than
60 independent views. This does not mean the class never disagrees: weaker negative
correlations exist (31 negative edges at $|r| \geq 0.10$, 5 at $0.20$, 3 at $0.25$), but none
reaches the 0.30 threshold.

\textbf{One broad component and three detached statements.} 57 of the 60 statements (95\%)
lie in a single connected component, so the network is not split into separate, equally sized
groups of statements. The remaining three are isolated, i.e.\ none correlates with any other
statement at $|r| \geq 0.30$: E02 (written exams measure knowledge accurately), E03
(compulsory attendance) and E04 (online learning complementing classroom teaching). These are
exactly the three statements found to be the most contested in the User--Statement analysis
(highest std, Section~5.3). This suggests that where the class divides on these Education
issues is largely unrelated to how respondents answer the rest of the survey.

\textbf{Hubs and bridges.} S09 (universities should promote an inclusive, respectful
environment for differing viewpoints) has the highest degree (45 of a possible 59) and the
highest eigenvector centrality (0.1993): it co-varies with many statements, and those are
themselves well connected. S15 (public trust is essential for responsible adoption of
emerging technologies) is the only statement in the top five on all three measures: second in
degree (44), fourth in eigenvector centrality (0.1842) and first in betweenness (0.0479),
making it both a hub and the strongest bridge between regions of the network. Degree and
betweenness are not interchangeable: T07 (robots replacing humans in hazardous jobs) is
outside the degree top five yet third in betweenness, so its importance comes from where it
sits rather than from its number of links. That S09 is also one of the strongest consensus
statements (Section~6.3) shows that centrality here reflects co-variation with other
statements, not high average agreement.

\textbf{Structure cuts across the survey's categories.} The top-ranked statements come from
all four categories (S, E, V, T) rather than from one: for example, the Environment
statements V07, V14 and V15 sit alongside the Society statements S09 and S15 among the most
central nodes. Response patterns therefore link statements across the survey's own T/E/S/V
boundaries. This is evidence of cross-category association in how respondents answered, not
of the original categories being wrong.

\textbf{Robustness and caveats.} S09, S15, V07, V14 and V15 repeatedly appear among the most
central statements across the thresholds tried, so their prominence does not depend entirely
on the single choice $\tau = 0.30$, although all findings remain conditional on it. The network describes
associations between response patterns, not causal influence: a highly connected or
high-betweenness statement is not necessarily one the class strongly agrees with, nor one that
drives opinions on other issues.

\subsection{User--Statement Network}
The near-total density (98.8\%) combined with a positive mean edge weight ($+1.14$) shows the class
leans broadly toward agreement across the survey, consistent with the 80\%/13\%/7\%
positive/neutral/negative split (Section~5.3): most respondents answered most statements, and most
answers were Agree or Strongly Agree.

At the respondent level, mean\_response isolates a small set of genuine outliers from the agreeable
majority. Respondent 120 has the lowest mean\_response ($-0.21$), the only respondent whose average
stance falls on the disagreement side of neutral despite answering 56 of 60 statements. That looks
like a real dissenting voice rather than a low-engagement artifact. Notably, this is the same
respondent (120) flagged as the primary out-strength outlier in the User--User network
(Section~6.1), corroborating across networks that respondent 120 is a genuine opinion outlier
rather than an artifact of either method.

At the statement level, std\_response identifies the Education-category statements E02, E03, and E04
as the most contested in the entire survey, while E15 and V13 are the strongest consensus statements.
It's worth pointing out that \textbf{S09}, the most structurally central statement in the
Statement--Statement network (highest degree and eigenvector centrality), also shows up among the
most consensus statements here (mean $1.47$, std $0.59$). So S09's centrality seems to come from
\emph{broad shared agreement} with many other statements rather than from controversy: centrality
and polarization are measuring two different things.
